# dog friendly hotels: how to filter out listings that only tolerate dogs, check the real pet fee before you pay, and earn cashback on the room

"Pet friendly" is one of the least reliable labels in travel. A hotel can tick that box because it accepts a 10-pound lapdog in one ground-floor room at an extra £40 a night, and the listing will look exactly as welcoming as a property that hands your labrador a bed and a bowl at no charge. Booking.com's own guidance is blunt about it: pet friendly does not automatically mean there is no pet fee, and pet fees typically run £10–50 per night, sometimes per pet, sometimes as a one-off cleaning charge.

So the search itself is a two-part job. First, work out which properties genuinely suit your dog. Second, stop overpaying for the room, because the pet fee is rarely the biggest number on the bill. This guide covers both, including how to route the booking through ShopBack so a percentage of what you spend on the stay comes back.

## What the pet friendly filter actually tells you

Search filters are necessary but shallow. Booking.com says 8 million users used its pet filter in 2023, which is a lot of demand chasing a checkbox that is not standardised. The filter tells you a property has a pet policy. It does not tell you the size of your dog, the number of pets, the price, or whether you can leave the dog in the room while you eat dinner.

Two features are worth knowing about:

- **"Pets stay free" badges.** Where a property does not charge for pets, Booking.com surfaces a badge during search, which lets you filter out anything with a fee attached.
- **Welcome Pets rate plans.** Booking.com launched pet-friendly rate plans in April 2024 so hosts can build the pet charge into the displayed room price. The point was to stop guests discovering the pet fee at check-in, and it is a genuinely useful filter when you are comparing total cost.

One more detail from Booking.com's guest help pages: where a vacation rental listing shows no pet fee and the description says nothing further, the default is up to two pets without needing to check with the host first. That is a floor, not a guarantee, and it is exactly the kind of detail people only discover when they are already standing at the door.

If you travel with a service animal, that is a separate category. Booking.com's guidance states service animals are legally protected and cannot be charged a pet fee. For anything else, from an emotional support animal to a cat, assume the property's own rules apply and confirm in writing.

## How to search without opening forty tabs

The process that wastes the least time looks roughly like this.

1. Search your destination and dates on Booking.com, then open the filters and go to the Facilities section and tick "Pets allowed". You can also select "Travelling with pets?" in the guest selector before you search, which applies the same filter from the start.
2. Look for properties showing a no-fee or pets-stay-free badge, and compare the total: nightly rate plus pet fee, multiplied by the number of nights. A £25 pet fee on a five-night stay is £125, which can flip the ranking of two hotels.
3. Read the property's own pet policy, not the summary on the booking site. Booking site summaries lag behind policy changes, and the property's own page is the version that will be enforced at check-in.
4. Cross-check dedicated directories if the property looks borderline. Sites built specifically around pet-friendly accommodation, such as BringFido and Pets Welcome, tend to publish weight limits and fees in one place, though some of their policy data runs old. Treat them as a lead generator, not a source of truth.
5. Message the property with specifics rather than asking a yes/no question. Breed, weight, number of dogs, whether the dog is crate-trained. Vague questions get vague answers.
6. Save the reply. A written confirmation is what you show at the front desk if a clerk reads the policy differently.

Expedia, Hotels.com, Priceline and Agoda all have their own pet filters, and the same rules apply: filter first, verify second, book third.

## The five questions that decide whether the stay actually works

The pet fee is the first question, but not the only one that ruins trips.

**Is the fee per night, per stay, or a refundable deposit?** The three structures produce wildly different totals over a week. Ask which one applies, and whether it is charged on arrival or at booking.

**Is there a weight or breed limit, and is it enforced?** Booking.com notes that properties sometimes offer reduced rates for smaller pets, using roughly 24 pounds as a dividing line. If your dog is over that, the limit matters more than the fee, because being turned away is the worst version of this problem.

**How many pets can you bring?** Per-pet pricing is common. Two dogs can double a nightly charge, and some properties cap the total.

**Can the dog be left alone in the room?** This is the question people forget and then spend the whole holiday working around. Some properties require the dog to be crated if you leave; others prohibit leaving the pet at all. If your plan involves a museum or a long dinner, you need this answered before you book.

**Where can the dog go on the property?** A hotel can allow pets and still exclude them from the restaurant, the pool deck and every patch of grass you can see. Ask about the designated relief area and how far it is from your room.

## Matching the property type to your dog

Pet policies cluster by property type, and knowing the pattern saves time.

**Chain hotels** win on consistency. A brand policy applies across locations, so once you know how a chain handles dogs, you know it for every city on the itinerary. What you give up is flexibility. The rules are the rules, usually at the front desk, usually at 11pm when you arrive.

**Boutique hotels and B&Bs** vary more but bend more. Individual owners can waive a fee for a repeat guest or a small dog, and they are more likely to answer a direct question honestly.

**Vacation rentals** suit dogs with energy. Booking.com's own partner research found travellers with pets often choose rentals over hotels for the extra space, private outdoor areas and a more relaxed environment. A fenced garden beats a hotel corridor for most dogs. Rentals also tend to have clearer fee structures, though the cleaning fee does some of the work a pet fee would.
